Vocal2Piano: End-to-End Autonomous Robotic Piano Accompaniment System

Embedded Systems | PCB | CAD | React/TypeScript | Music Information Retrieval | Robotics | Web Serial

overview.

Vocal2Piano is a robot that sits on a real piano and accompanies a singer live. A React and TypeScript web app turns a MIDI file, sheet music, a hummed tune, or a chord loop into a full piano arrangement, works out every hand move and key press the robot needs to play it, and streams that plan to a Teensy 4.1 over USB a fraction of a second ahead of time. Each of the robot's two hands is a 13-solenoid carriage sliding on a stepper-driven belt, built around a pair of custom PCBs and a Fusion 360 mechanical design, with firmware that enforces its own current, timing, and collision limits no matter what the app sends it.

System

Vocal2Piano is a two-handed robot that sits on top of a real piano and accompanies a singer live. Each hand is a carriage of 13 solenoid fingers (7 white-key, 6 black-key) that slides along the keyboard on a GT2 belt driven by a NEMA17 stepper, so 26 fingers in total cover the full playing range. A web app prepares the song, works out every hand move and key press, and streams them to the robot over USB while the song plays — once a performance starts, the robot runs on its own.

The assembled robot in its enclosure — bass hand on the left, treble hand on the right

Planning a performance

A song enters the web app as a MIDI file, sheet music (MusicXML), a tune hummed into the microphone, or a built-in chord loop. From there it can be adjusted — key, speed, piano style, chords, and individual bars or notes — with every change audible on the computer's speakers immediately.

The app then plans the robot's half of the job: where each hand needs to be at every moment, which finger takes each note, when every move and strike has to happen, and how hard each key should be struck. It reports what share of the song the robot can physically play and lists anything it can't. Once the robot is connected and homed over USB, Play streams that plan a fraction of a second ahead of time, and the firmware executes it on schedule. With Listen on, the piano also tracks the singer as it plays: it waits for each entrance, stops when the singing stops, matches the singer's loudness, and follows the key being sung.

Hardware

One C0nTrollER board carries the Teensy 4.1, two TMC2209 stepper drivers, and the power input stage; two identical PLAyeRs boards, one per hand, each carry a PCA9685 driving 13 logic-level MOSFET channels over I²C. The two PLAyeRs boards share a single I²C bus and are told apart by a solder jumper — bridged for treble (0x41), open for bass (0x40).

Power comes in as 12 V over a barrel jack rated for 5 A, through a slow-blow fuse, an SMBJ16A TVS diode for spike clamping, and a P-MOSFET reverse-polarity guard, into a 4700 µF bulk capacitor that keeps the rail steady when motors and solenoids switch. An MP1584 buck converter steps that down to 5 V for the logic. Both boards are 4-layer, with the inner layers dedicated to ground and +12 V planes — wide, low-resistance paths for the solenoid and motor current that also keep motor noise away from the logic.

Striking a key

Each solenoid channel is a PCA9685 PWM output through a 100 Ω gate resistor into an IRLZ34N MOSFET acting as a low-side switch, with a 10 kΩ pull-down holding it off by default, an SS14 diode absorbing the inductive kick when the coil switches off, and a status LED that lights on every strike. The solenoids themselves (JF-1039(B), 12 V, about 0.4 A, 10 mm stroke) deliver up to 25 N at full closure, with much less force earlier in the stroke.

With all 13 channels on one board firing at once, that board alone draws about 5.2 A — both boards together would need roughly 10 A, more than the 5 A supply can give. The firmware caps total solenoid current at 4 A and skips any strike that would cross it, and the web app's planner works within the same budget. A strike itself has three phases: full duty for 50 ms to attack the note, fully on (silent) until 300 ms in, then a 35 % hold duty until the note ends, at most 2 s after it started. Black-key fingers need much less force, so their channels are scaled to 40 % of whatever duty was requested.

Carriages and firmware

Each hand's 13 fingers ride together on one carriage, moved along the keyboard by a NEMA17 stepper on a GT2 belt — the whole assembly, enclosure included, is 1676 × 215 × 123 mm, modeled in Fusion 360.

Carriage, exploded

The Teensy firmware keeps the hardware code (main.cpp: a 50 kHz stepper interrupt, the PCA9685s, the end stops, USB) separate from the protocol, the time-sorted command queue, and the safety rules (core.cpp), which run unmodified against a simulated robot in tests. Moves and strikes arrive 0.1–0.3 s before they're due and sit in the queue until their timestamp comes up, so a whole performance never has to fit on the device at once.

Whatever the app sends, the firmware enforces its own limits: no solenoid stays on past 2 s, no more than 4 A of solenoid current at once, and the two hands always keep at least 5 free white keys between them. A move past either end of the keyboard, or one that would close that gap, is refused outright, speed is capped at 100 mm/s and acceleration at 1000 mm/s², and a closed connection or a pulled USB cable is treated exactly like an emergency stop.

Musical intelligence

A hummed tune is tracked with probabilistic YIN, which resolves octave ambiguity with a hidden Markov model. Every incoming note updates a 12-bin pitch-class histogram that decays 2 % per note, and on every eighth note the system correlates that histogram against the Krumhansl–Kessler major and minor key profiles for all 24 keys — the key estimate settles after about four bars and follows modulations within a few notes of the change.

From there, a Markov chain over Roman-numeral chords (I ii iii IV V vi vii) picks the next chord once per bar, biased 2.5× toward chords that contain the current melody note. The default transition weights are hand-tuned, but a training script can replace them with values learned from any folder of MIDI files. They, along with the harmonizer's melody weighting, were tuned against two public datasets: the McGill Billboard corpus (739 songs, also the source of the built-in demo loops) and POP909 (909 songs with melody and chord annotations). Measured on POP909, picking chords from melody and chord habits together gets the annotated chord right 39.1 % of the time per half bar — against 35.7 % from the melody alone and 28.6 % from chord habits alone.

The arrangement is rendered in one of five styles — block chords, broken chords, Alberti bass, stride, or bossa nova — and with Listen on, the same pitch tracking follows the live singer: waiting for each entrance, stopping when the singing stops, matching loudness, and moving to the key being sung.
